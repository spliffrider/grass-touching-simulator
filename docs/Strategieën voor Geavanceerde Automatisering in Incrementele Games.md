To make the automation in "Grass Touching Simulator" feel engaging and deep, you can draw from several advanced design concepts used in some of the most successful incremental games. Relying purely on basic "hire a gardener to generate 10 grass per second" mechanics will eventually cause the game to lose momentum.  
Here are some strategies to add depth and improve the "feel" of your automation systems:  
**1\. Implement "Derivative" Systems (Generator-Generators)**Instead of having all your automation solely produce "Grass," you can introduce higher tiers of automation that produce the lower tiers 1, 2\. For example:

* **Tier 1:** "Amateur Gardeners" touch grass (produces Primary Currency).  
* **Tier 2:** "Gardening Schools" produce Amateur Gardeners.  
* **Tier 3:** "Real Estate Developers" build Gardening Schools.

Because each parent generator acts as the rate of change for the child generator, this creates a mathematical "Taylor series expansion" 3, 4\. As you add more tiers, the resource generation naturally approaches an explosive, exponential growth curve, which feels incredibly satisfying to the player 3\.  
**2\. Use Milestone Multipliers to Keep Early Automation Relevant**In many games, early-game automation becomes completely useless once late-game systems are unlocked 5, 6\. To fix this, you can introduce milestone bonuses. For example, like in *Clicker Heroes* or *AdVenture Capitalist*, you can apply a massive x4 speed or production multiplier every time the player buys 25, 50, or 100 units of a specific generator 5, 7\. This turns the act of buying cheap, early-game automation into a strategic goal rather than just dead weight 8\.  
**3\. Cap Resources to Create "Active-Idle" Loops**If automation simply runs forever, players might close the game and forget about it. To fix this, consider taking inspiration from *Orb of Creation* by adding upgradeable resource caps 9, 10\. Once your grass silos (or whatever storage you use) are full, the automation pauses. This creates an engaging loop where the game is idle until the cap is hit, prompting the player to return, spend their resources to expand their caps or buy new upgrades, and then let the automation resume 9\.  
**4\. Add "Familiarity" or Time-Loop Mechanics**Instead of a standard prestige where you just earn a flat multiplier, you could try a time-loop system similar to the game *Increlution* 11, 12\. In this system, every time the player "prestiges" or loops, the actions and automations they completed in the previous run take less time to execute because their character "remembers" how to do it 12, 13\. The more you automate a specific task across multiple runs, the faster and more efficient it naturally becomes.  
**5\. Scriptable or Programmable Automation**For late-game depth, you can allow players to write the "rules" of their own automation. Games like *Stone Story RPG* use simple pseudocode, while *Bitburner* allows players to use actual JavaScript to automate complex market actions 14-16. You could unlock a "Lawn Maintenance Script" feature where players set up "If/Then" parameters (e.g., *If Grass \> 10,000, Auto-Buy Sprinkler*), rewarding them for clever logical setups 15\.  
**6\. Balancing the "Feel" of the Progression Wall**Math alone will not make your automation fun 17, 18\. To nail the pacing:

* **Do the 1-Hour Test:** Don't try to balance the whole game at once. Implement a single automation loop and playtest it for an hour. You will immediately feel if you are waiting too long for the next upgrade, or if the automation is so fast that you're never waiting at all 19, 20\.  
* **Map it in a Spreadsheet:** To ensure your costs and automation payouts scale well, use spreadsheets (like Excel) or graphing tools (like Desmos) to plot the cost-to-income ratios 21, 22\. You want to visually ensure that upgrade costs will occasionally outpace the player's automated income, intentionally creating a "progression wall" that forces them to think strategically or perform a prestige reset 1, 23\.
